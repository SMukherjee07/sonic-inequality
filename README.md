## Overview

Noise pollution is often treated as an environmental nuisance, but chronic exposure may be unevenly distributed across communities and potentially associated with health, socioeconomic conditions, and economic mobility.

**Sonic Inequality** investigates how environmental noise exposure varies across U.S. census tracts and examines its relationship with population health and intergenerational economic mobility.

The project combines environmental, demographic, socioeconomic, health, and geographic data to investigate three connected questions:

1. **Who bears the greatest noise burden?**
2. **How is noise exposure associated with health outcomes?**
3. **Is noise exposure associated with differences in intergenerational economic mobility?**

The analysis moves from descriptive and regression-based methods toward nonlinear, spatial, and causal inference frameworks.

---

## Research Questions

### 1. Environmental Justice

Are noise burdens distributed unequally across communities after accounting for socioeconomic and geographic characteristics?

### 2. Health

Is environmental noise exposure associated with hypertension, cardiovascular disease, depression, and other health outcomes?

### 3. Nonlinear Effects

Are relationships between noise exposure and health adequately described by linear models, or do threshold effects emerge at higher exposure levels?

### 4. Spatial Dependence

Do neighboring communities exhibit correlated health outcomes after accounting for observed socioeconomic and environmental characteristics?

### 5. Economic Mobility

Is noise exposure associated with intergenerational income mobility after accounting for established socioeconomic predictors?

### 6. Causal Identification

Can plausibly exogenous changes in aviation noise generated by runway expansions be used to estimate a causal effect of increased noise exposure on hypertension?

# Data

The project integrates multiple public datasets at the U.S. census-tract level.

| Dataset | Role |
|---|---|
| **U.S. Census ACS** | Income, poverty, education, housing, race/ethnicity, population |
| **CDC PLACES** | Hypertension, coronary heart disease, depression, sleep, obesity |
| **EPA EJScreen** | Aviation-related environmental exposure measures |
| **DOT National Transportation Noise Map** | Road and rail noise exposure |
| **Opportunity Insights** | Intergenerational economic mobility |
| **FAA Airport Records** | Airport/runway information for causal analysis |

The primary observational analysis uses census tracts as the unit of analysis.

# Feature Engineering

The project constructs several analytical variables from the raw datasets.

### Composite Noise Index

Noise exposure from aviation, road, and rail sources is standardized and combined into a composite index:

\[
NoiseIndex =
\frac{Z_{aviation}+Z_{road}+Z_{rail}}{3}
\]

This provides a common measure of overall environmental noise burden across transportation sources.

### Socioeconomic Variables

The analysis derives measures including:

- Median household income
- Poverty rate
- Educational attainment
- Renter share
- Population density
- Urban/rural classification
- Racial and ethnic composition

# Statistical Methods

The project deliberately progresses from relatively simple models toward more flexible and structurally appropriate approaches.

### Exploratory Data Analysis

- Distribution analysis
- Missingness analysis
- Correlation analysis
- Geographic visualization
- Environmental justice comparisons

### Multiple Linear Regression

Used to investigate socioeconomic predictors of noise exposure and associations between noise and health outcomes.

Model diagnostics include:

- Residual diagnostics
- Breusch–Pagan tests
- Variance inflation factors
- Robust standard errors
- Relative importance analysis

### ANOVA / ANCOVA

Used to examine whether health outcomes differ across dominant noise-source classifications while adjusting for socioeconomic characteristics.

### Generalized Additive Models

GAMs are used to investigate potential nonlinear relationships between environmental noise and health outcomes using penalized spline functions.

### Generalized Linear Models

The project explores alternative outcome distributions using:

- Poisson regression
- Negative binomial regression
- Beta regression

### Spatial Regression

Spatial dependence is investigated using:

- Moran's I
- Spatial weights
- Spatial lag models (SAR)
- Spatial error models (SEM)
- Direct and indirect effects

### Intergenerational Mobility

Linear models and relative-importance analysis examine the association between environmental noise and intergenerational income mobility while accounting for socioeconomic characteristics.

### Causal Inference

The final analysis explores a staggered difference-in-differences design based on FAA runway expansions.

The Callaway–Sant'Anna framework is used to investigate treatment effects across multiple runway expansion events and to examine pre-treatment trends and robustness to alternative control groups.

Key Findings

The current analysis investigates evidence for several patterns:

Unequal distribution of environmental noise across demographic and socioeconomic groups
Differences in health outcomes across dominant noise-source categories
Potential nonlinear relationships between road noise and hypertension
Spatial dependence in tract-level health outcomes
Associations between environmental noise and intergenerational economic mobility
Changes in hypertension following plausibly exogenous runway expansion events

The complete statistical results, diagnostics, visualizations, and model specifications are available in the master notebook.

Important: These are observational and tract-level analyses unless otherwise specified. Associations should not automatically be interpreted as individual-level or causal effects.

Why This Project?

Environmental exposures rarely occur independently of social and economic conditions.

A community's exposure to transportation noise can be related simultaneously to:

income
housing
urbanization
racial composition
transportation infrastructure
environmental policy
health
economic opportunity

This project therefore treats noise not simply as an environmental variable, but as a potential component of spatial inequality.

The goal is to combine statistical modeling with environmental and socioeconomic data to investigate these relationships while explicitly accounting for confounding, spatial dependence, nonlinearities, and causal-identification challenges.

Limitations

Several limitations are central to interpreting the analysis:

Census-tract observations are ecological and do not directly represent individual-level effects.
CDC PLACES health estimates are model-based estimates rather than individual clinical measurements.
Environmental noise measurements and contours involve measurement/modeling uncertainty.
Cross-sectional analyses cannot establish temporal causality.
Socioeconomic and environmental variables may remain subject to omitted-variable bias.
Intergenerational mobility measures correspond to historical birth cohorts and may not align temporally with contemporary environmental measurements.
Spatial and causal models depend on assumptions that require diagnostic and sensitivity analysis.

These limitations are treated as part of the research question rather than as an afterthought.
